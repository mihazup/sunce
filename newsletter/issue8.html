Step Into the Tanryū Dojo

Protecting the desire to learn

Nanbu Doshu Soke shared his own principles to guide a life (jinseikun):

  • Shogai Keiko — continue to practice throughout one's life.
  • Shogai Yuki — base one's life on courage and perseverance.
  • Shogai Shinnen — lead a life guided by one's convictions.

To me, these principles are a reminder that Nanbudo does not end at the next grading. It is a lifelong journey.

That became especially meaningful when I started teaching our youngest practitioners. I remember one child who had recently started school. His days had suddenly become longer, filled with structured learning, expectations and new impressions. One evening he arrived at training completely drained. He followed my instructions, but the curiosity and joy I was used to seeing were gone. He was physically present, but not truly in the training.

As instructors, it is easy to look at a syllabus and see everything that still needs to be covered. Another randori, another kata, another requirement before the next belt. But the child standing in front of us may already have spent an entire day being told what to learn, when to concentrate and what is expected of them.

I believe children should fall in love with training before the focus shifts from discovery to achievement. Which led me to a question that continues to shape the way I teach today:

What does this child need from me today in order to still want to learn tomorrow?

Over time, that question became closely connected to my personal jinseikun: Never lose the desire to learn.

I have come to wonder whether lifelong learning depends on more than knowledge itself. Perhaps it also requires curiosity, the courage to ask questions, the freedom to make mistakes and the willingness to explore.

If we hope children will continue practicing throughout their lives, maybe one of our responsibilities is to protect the desire that makes them want to return. If we want them to develop courage and perseverance, perhaps they also need room to struggle, fail and discover that effort can lead to mastery.

I do not see this as a definitive answer to how children should be taught. It is simply a reflection shaped by my own experiences, and one that continues to evolve.

Changing the focus, not removing the syllabus

This philosophy has also changed how we approach the syllabus and grading.

The syllabus has not disappeared, and neither have our technical expectations. What has changed is when the syllabus becomes the focus and how the children encounter it.

For our youngest practitioners, I would rather spend time developing balance, coordination, mobility, body awareness and an understanding of individual techniques than rush through longer patterns simply because they appear on the next grading syllabus.

The children still meet the stances, Japanese terminology, techniques and principles they will eventually need. We simply allow them to develop these skills gradually and through different experiences before asking them to reproduce longer sequences.

We have also moved away from traditional gradings for the youngest children. Before the age of ten, we instead finish the term with a showcase where they demonstrate what they have been working on and receive their semester stripes.

My intention is to give the children goals to work towards, while first building a foundation of genuine joy towards training. I want them to associate Nanbudo with curiosity, effort, mastery and the desire to keep learning. When formal grading eventually begins, I want each belt to reflect what they genuinely know, understand and can do.

During these first years, I want the focus to remain on learning rather than passing.

My hope is that when formal grading eventually becomes part of their Nanbudo journey, much of the material already feels familiar because the foundations have been developing over time.

Welcome to our dojo

My name is Linda Nataliye, and I am the head instructor of Tanryū, our shortest dragons at Skien Nanbudoklubb in Norway.

Together with my fellow instructors, I try to create a dojo where our youngest practitioners can explore, challenge themselves and develop a genuine love for Nanbudo.

So, come inside our dojo. Let me show you what that looks like in practice.

Becoming part of a dojo

When the children arrive, training has already begun in a small but meaningful way.

They bow as they enter, greet each other and help prepare the tatami. The brooms are ready when they arrive, and we prepare the dojo together.

There is always room for laughter and play, but also clear expectations around respect, responsibility and how we treat one another. I want the children to understand that they are not simply attending a class, they are becoming part of a dojo with its own traditions, values and shared responsibilities.

This is also why Tatami-Talk is a fixed part of our training.

We sit together in a circle and discuss a chosen topic. Often, we talk about dojo etiquette, Nanbudo's history and traditions, or how we create a safe and joyful training environment.

I introduce the subject, but the conversation is not meant to be one-way. The children are encouraged to ask questions, share their thoughts and listen to each other.

Instead of simply telling them to bow, listen or help clean the dojo, we can also discuss why we do these things.

For me, Tatami-Talk creates room for learning that is not purely physical. We practice communication, reflection, empathy and responsibility while also getting to know each other beyond our technical abilities.

It is one of the ways I try to show the children that Nanbudo is more than punches, kicks and falls.

Familiar routines, gradually developing focus

Another fixed element is the way we begin.

We start with Nanbudo Mitsu No Chikara and Ki Nanbu Taiso, creating a familiar transition from the outside world into the dojo.

I use this moment to help the children leave some of the day's stress behind and become present. Concentration is something we practice, it is not something I expect a six-year-old to have mastered yet.

The familiar routine seems to help them settle. They know what comes next, and gradually they learn to shift their attention towards the training ahead.

Ki Nanbu Taiso itself is taught slowly.

Rather than asking the youngest children to memorize the complete sequence, we divide it into smaller elements. We may spend time exploring Nami, the wave, before adding Kaze, the wind, followed later by Iwa and Matsu and so on.

We use movement, illustrations, demonstrations and conversation. The children see what a wave looks like, hear what the Japanese word means and then explore the movement with their own bodies.

By connecting what they see, hear and physically experience, I hope the movement becomes something they understand rather than a choreography they remember.

Over the course of a semester, these small elements gradually become the complete Ki Nanbu Taiso.

Dachi through movement and play

Dachi-Drill is another regular feature, although the stances and exercises may change from session to session.

Through short sequences and games, the children become familiar with both the Japanese names and the physical positions. As they develop, we gradually add balance, foot placement, posture and body control.

One example is our version of Red Light, Green Light.

When the spotter turns around, after counting to three in Japanese, of course, the children do not simply freeze. They must stop in a specific Nanbudo stance, or sometimes choose one themselves.

A familiar children's game suddenly becomes repetition of Japanese terminology, reaction time and technical positioning.

Another recurring favorite, or perhaps a love-hate favorite — is Bring Sally Up.

Instead of using the exercise only for squats or push-ups, we use different Nanbudo stances and movements. Shiko Dachi works well. Iwa is one of my own personal favorites.

The children do not necessarily share my enthusiasm for Iwa, which is probably why it keeps returning week after week.

Exploring technique

In the main part of the session, I often focus on a technical theme rather than longer patterns and sequences.

We may explore balance, strength, mobility and control, giving the children time to understand the qualities behind a technique rather than simply repeat movements.

One of their favorite exercises is what we call the Yoko Geri tunnel.

The children stand in a line and hold a controlled Yoko Geri while another child crawls underneath. To them, it feels like a game. To me, it creates an opportunity to work on balance, stability and control while giving me time to adjust their stance, offer individual feedback and encourage them to continue working.

The exercise is playful, but the technical purpose remains clear.

I often use questions and small experiments as part of technical training. Rather than only showing the children the "correct" way to move, we test what happens when we change something. What happens to the balance if the supporting foot moves? Does the technique feel stronger from one position than another? What changes if we slow the movement down, hold it longer, or adjust the stance?

The point is not to make every attempt successful. In fact, some of the most useful moments come when something does not work. I want the children to feel the difference, recognize what made the technique less effective, and then try again with a small adjustment.

This creates room for mistakes to become part of the learning process rather than something to avoid. Sometimes the children discover the answer themselves, sometimes I guide them, and sometimes we simply leave the question open and return to it another day.

For me, technical training is therefore not only about reproducing a movement correctly. It is also about exploring, testing, failing, adjusting and gradually developing a better understanding of why the technique works.

Learning does not have to happen all at once.

More than learning techniques

Ultimately, I hope this approach contributes to a higher technical level as the children grow older.

But that is not my only measure of success.

I want the children to experience what it feels like to work for something, struggle, make mistakes and eventually recognize their own progress. I want them to learn that "I can't" can become "I can't yet."

I want them to learn how to communicate with training partners, help younger children, take responsibility for their dojo and understand that making someone else better is also part of becoming a better martial artist.

And if one of my Tanryū children eventually chooses football, music or something completely different, I hope Nanbudo still follows them in some form, through the values they learned, their willingness to try, their ability to cope with mistakes and the confidence that mastery can grow from effort.

The syllabus will still be there.
The next grading will still be there.
But first, I want to give them a reason to come back tomorrow.

Because if we can preserve the desire to learn, there will always be time to learn more.
